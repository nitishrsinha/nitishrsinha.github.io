What to do

  1. Replicate the main result. Rebuild the paper’s main table: the portfolio sort, long-short spread, factor or Fama-MacBeth slope. Use the paper’s sample period and screens. Put your numbers next to the paper’s. Where they differ, say why, and what you tried to close the gap. A careful replication that explains a gap is worth more than a matching table you can’t explain.
  2. Test it after the sample ended. Extend the backtest through December 2025 (or the latest month in CRSP). Report the average monthly long-short return, its t-statistic and the Sharpe ratio in two periods: the paper’s sample, and the months after it. Did the strategy keep working? The box below explains how to read the answer.
  3. Give a verdict. Would you put money into this strategy today? What result would make you stop?
Background: the McLean–Pontiff test

McLean and Pontiff, Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability? (Journal of Finance, 2016; Week 3, Reading #14.5), ask what happens to a published anomaly after the paper’s sample ends. They study 97 predictors and split each one’s history into three periods:

  • In sample: the months the authors used.
  • Out of sample, before publication: after the sample ends but before the paper is published. Investors don’t know about the signal yet. A drop here points to data mining or luck: part of the in-sample return was never real.
  • After publication: once the paper is public. An extra drop here points to investors learning from the paper and trading the signal away.

Their test is one regression: the monthly long-short return on a dummy for the out-of-sample months and a dummy for the post-publication months. The constant is the in-sample average; each dummy’s coefficient is how much the return falls in that period. Across their 97 predictors, returns are about 26% lower out of sample and about 58% lower after publication.

Use this to read your step 2. If your strategy’s return fell after the sample ended, that is the typical pattern, not a failed replication. The question is how much it fell, and whether what is left is still worth trading. Running the regression yourself is optional.
